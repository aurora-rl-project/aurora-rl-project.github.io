Reinforcement Learning across Regions

SparrowRL trains large-model RL on cheap, cross-cloud GPUs wired together by ordinary 1–10 Gbps links, reaching the throughput of a dedicated RDMA supercluster by shipping only the ~1% of weights that actually change each step, losslessly.

Reinforcement learning is how today's strongest reasoning models are built. But the systems that run it quietly assume something most teams don't have: a rack of GPUs stitched together with hundred-gigabit RDMA. SparrowRL removes that assumption, and gets nearly the same speed over the kind of network you can rent on demand.

Who it's forDemocratizing RL training

For the last few years, RL post-training (the step that turns a capable base model into a strong reasoner) has effectively belonged to whoever could reserve a hundred-gigabit RDMA supercluster. The strange part: the GPUs themselves are no longer scarce. You can rent H100s and A100s by the hour from a dozen providers, across a dozen regions. What you can't cheaply rent is the tightly-coupled fabric that fuses them into one cluster, and without that fabric the weight-sync wall (next section) puts RL training out of reach. SparrowRL is about removing that last barrier, so serious RL post-training runs on the cheap, loosely-connected GPUs almost anyone can already get.

For academic labs and small teams, that turns "we can't get a reserved RDMA cluster" into a non-issue: stitch together on-demand GPUs from whatever clouds and regions are cheap and available, and run real large-model RL over the cross-region links between them, at near-RDMA throughput and a better price.

For industry, disaggregated RL is becoming the norm at scale: rather than one homogeneous cluster, training and rollout run on separate, specialized GPU pools. The two phases have opposite hardware appetites. Training is memory- and bandwidth-bound and wants top-end GPUs with fast interconnect; rollout generation is throughput-bound and runs fine on cheaper accelerators. Splitting them lets each pool be right-sized and scaled independently: add rollout actors on cheap or spot GPUs without touching the trainer, and keep the expensive training GPUs busy instead of idling through generation. What makes this impractical today is the link between the pools, and that is exactly the bottleneck SparrowRL's sparse-delta sync removes, without the WAN tax that normally makes the split a non-starter.

RL post-training follows a simple loop: a Trainer updates the policy, then broadcasts the new weights to a fleet of Rollout Actors that generate samples and send them back. The catch is that broadcast. Because RL refreshes the entire model every single step, the weight transfer sits squarely on the critical path, and it scales with model size. On a datacenter RDMA fabric this is invisible. On a cross-region network, it is the whole story.

The wallWhy RL needs a supercomputer

Consider an 8B model. In BF16 that's ~16 GB of weights that must reach every actor, every step. On a 100 Gbps RDMA link the sync takes about 1.3 seconds, comfortably hidden inside the ~45-second window the actors spend generating. Move the same job to a 1 Gbps cross-cloud link and that sync balloons to 128 seconds, dwarfing generation entirely. Even one-step asynchronous pipelining can't hide a transfer that's longer than the work it's supposed to overlap with, and GPU utilization collapses into the single digits.

This is why state-of-the-art RL systems (OpenRLHF, veRL, StreamRL) are built for tightly-coupled RDMA clusters. And those clusters are exactly what most of the field can't easily get:

  • A network premium. RDMA-connected nodes cost more than plain Ethernet — e.g. ~$39/hr vs ~$31/hr for 16×H100 on one provider, a 26% surcharge for the fabric alone.
  • Minimum commitments. Providers gate these clusters behind 24-hour to one-week reservations, so an exploratory 48-hour run can inflate into a one-week bill — up to 3.5× the cost you needed.
  • Procurement gatekeeping. Reserving large RDMA blocks often requires sales relationships and credit lines that academic labs, government groups, and startups simply don't have.

Meanwhile, the same 16 GPUs stitched together on-demand from two clouds run ~$26–27/hr with 1-hour billing. The capacity is right there, sitting behind cross-region links of 1–10 Gbps. The only thing standing between that cheap, abundant capacity and real RL training is the weight-sync wall. So we asked: can you get RDMA-class RL throughput over a cross-region network?

The insightRL barely changes the model

The usual response to a bandwidth problem is to throw more bandwidth at it. We took the opposite view: stop treating the RL loop as a black box, open it up, and look at what is actually being sent. The answer is striking. Unlike pretraining, where gradient descent nudges nearly every parameter every step, a single RL update leaves the overwhelming majority of weights bit-for-bit unchanged.

Measuring the element-wise nonzero ratio (the fraction of scalar parameters that differ between consecutive checkpoints), only about 1% of elements actually change value per step. The other 99% stay bit-for-bit identical. The update is fine-grained-sparse: almost every tensor sees some change, but within each tensor only a tiny scatter of elements is touched.

Why is RL so sparse? First, post-training uses learning rates around 1e-6 (two orders of magnitude below pretraining), so each step nudges weights only slightly, refining behavior without overwriting knowledge. Second, RL's updates are in-distribution, which recent theory shows resists forgetting and induces smaller parameter changes than supervised fine-tuning, while regularizers like KL penalties and gradient clipping actively bound how far each step can move. Lastly, and decisively, the precision the actors run in erases the rest. Rollout actors never load the trainer's FP32 master weights; they load BF16 checkpoints, whose resolution near a weight of size |W| is only about |W|/128. Most one-step updates land in the 10⁻⁷–10⁻⁶ range, well below that spacing at the typical weight scale of 10⁻³–10⁻¹, so when two consecutive checkpoints are rounded to BF16 and differenced, the overwhelming majority of updates collapse to exactly zero. Of the parameters that did change in FP32, only 1.27% survive into the BF16 delta the actor actually sees. The sparsity isn't just that RL updates are small; it's that they're small enough to vanish into the precision the actors already use.

The opportunity → A full-weight broadcast spends ~99% of its bytes re-transmitting weights that didn't change. Send the difference instead, and send it losslessly, so training dynamics are untouched.

The systemHow SparrowRL works

SparrowRL turns that one observation into a system, built on top of PRIME-RL (PyTorch FSDP2 for training, vLLM for inference) in ~4.3K lines of Python, with no changes to the underlying RL algorithm. Three mechanisms work together.

1 · Lossless sparse deltas

Instead of an ephemeral broadcast, every step produces a versioned, immutable artifact that captures only the changed elements at full precision — no quantization, no dropped updates, so actors receive exactly the bits the trainer computed. Unifying checkpoint storage and network transfer into one object means a partial or retried transfer is never ambiguous: a rollout either matches a verified version hash or it doesn't.

Naively, each change is an (index, value) pair, and the 4-byte index dwarfs the 2-byte value. SparrowRL compresses the position metadata twice over: it stores differences between sorted indices, then encodes those differences with LEB128 variable-length integers: small gaps take a single byte, rare large gaps spill into a second. The index footprint drops from 4 bytes to under 2 per entry, shaving another 30–50% off the checkpoint.

2 · Streaming transfer

Sparse deltas are decomposable, so SparrowRL never waits for a whole file. It packetizes each delta into segments and starts shipping the first segment while the rest is still being extracted — cut-through forwarding from trainer to relay to actor. Segments are striped round-robin across several TCP streams, so a loss-induced stall on one stream doesn't block the others and the WAN link stays saturated (single-stream transfer of a 202 MB delta drops from 4.71 s to 2.90 s). Crucially, all of this overlaps with rollout generation: actors stage the next version in a buffer while still generating on the current one, then commit it in-place via sparse scatter-add at a safe point between batches. A rollout is never served from a half-applied model.

3 · Scheduling for messy fleets

Loosely-coupled actors differ by 2–3× in speed (H100 vs A100 vs L40), and those gaps drift as links congest and spot instances vanish. SparrowRL sizes each actor's share of the batch in proportion to its measured throughput (an EMA of tokens/s), so a fast H100 at 5,000 tok/s and an A100 at 2,500 tok/s split a 300-prompt batch 200/100 and finish together. A single feedback signal absorbs every kind of slowdown (GPU throttling, network congestion, contention) without tracking them separately. Version-aware gating keeps stale actors from poisoning a step, and time-bounded leases handle failure implicitly: if an actor (or a whole partitioned region) misses its deadline, its prompts simply return to the pool for healthy actors to absorb, with no global barrier and no manual recovery. Results are admitted only if their lease is still valid and their version and checkpoint hash match.

EvidenceDoes it hold up?

The evaluation runs Qwen3 4B/8B/14B with the Trainer on H100s in the US and rollout actors on A100/L40 GPUs in Canada, Japan, the Netherlands, and Iceland, over a real cross-cloud link that fluctuates between ~500 Mbps and 1 Gbps. Baselines: a vanilla cross-DC baseline (full-weight broadcast), its multi-stream variant, and Ideal-SingleDC (an idealized 800 Gbps RDMA upper bound). Each step now ships a 202 MB sparse delta instead of 15.6 GB of full weights — a 79× cut — and even at 0.25 Gbps that delta crosses in seconds, where a full broadcast needs 566 s.

No accuracy tax

Because every update is bit-exact, a SparrowRL-trained model is byte-for-byte identical to one trained with full-weight sync, so downstream accuracy isn't merely close, it's the same number. On the held-out reasoning benchmarks:

And because the compression is lossless (bit-exact updates, a preserved one-step policy lag), none of this trades away model quality, unlike approaches built on quantized gradient compression or aggressive staleness. You get the throughput of an expensive network on a cheap one, and the model never knows the difference.
